At a glance

Clarinet and piano. The first movement is the most accessible of the four-movement sonata (Henle level 7 overall; Allegretto challenges are chiefly legato, tone, dynamic shaping, and 12/8 chamber coordination). Suits advanced high-school players studying refined French style, and college students preparing the full sonata as standard recital/jury repertoire.

Composer & composition

Composer & late wind sonatas

Charles-Camille Saint-Saëns (9 October 1835, Paris – 16 December 1921, Algiers) was a French composer, pianist, and organist, a child prodigy and lifelong stylistic conservative. In the last year of his life he wrote three wind sonatas for instruments he felt had been neglected: Oboe Sonata Op. 166, Clarinet Sonata Op. 167, and Bassoon Sonata Op. 168. In a letter to Jean Chantavoine dated 15 April 1921 he wrote that he was concentrating his “last reserves on giving rarely considered instruments the chance to be heard.” He had intended further sonatas for cor anglais and flute but did not live to complete the set.

The Clarinet Sonata was written in 1921, completed with the other two by June, printed in November 1921 only a month before his death at 86. He did not live to hear the premieres. Dedicated to Auguste Périer, clarinet professor at the Conservatoire de Paris. Jean Gallois called it the most important of the three — “a masterpiece full of impishness, elegance and discreet lyricism.” Henle notes the wind sonatas “hark back to the gallant style of the 18th century and thus approach the neoclassicism movement around 1920,” marked by “humour, wit and fine irony.”

About movement I (Allegretto)

The sonata has four movements and lasts about 16 minutes; the first-movement theme returns at the end of the fourth. Movement I is Allegretto in E♭ major, 12/8. The opening melody floats over a “sea of utterly calm eighth-note waves in the piano”; the form is ternary with a somewhat more active middle section oscillating around C minor. The clarinet enters as an anacrusis on the fourth beat of the first measure.

Structure / listening map

  1. I. Allegretto

    Allegretto · E♭ major, 12/8

    Ternary form; pastoral, expansive theme over continuous piano eighth-notes; more active middle around C minor.

  2. II. Allegro animato

    Allegro animato · A♭ major, 2/2

    Second movement of the full sonata (context).

  3. III. Lento

    Lento · E♭ minor, 3/2

    Slow movement of the full sonata (context).

  4. IV. Molto allegro (– Allegretto)

    Molto allegro · E♭ major, 4/4

    Virtuosic finale; first-movement theme returns at the close. Often listed at Grade 8 technical demand.

Performance notes

French Romantic / gallant style

Aim for impishness, elegance and discreet lyricism — nostalgic, “autumnal” warmth rather than heavy late-Romantic weight. Prefer clear, refined French-school sound; use vibrato sparingly and naturally.

  • G. Henle Verlag
  • Interlude

Phrasing and rhythmic weighting

Saint-Saëns felt beat one should be stronger than beat two. Keep 12/8 flowing and pastoral; let the theme sing expansively rather than drag. Slight ritenuti only; let the music determine nuance choices.

  • Saint-Saëns Sonatas performance notes

Common mistakes

Treating the opening as “waiting for the hard part” → create motion and micro-phrase conversation. Losing subdivision → constant internal eighths + score study. Starting diminuendo too soft → crescendo-then-relax. Over-vibrating / heavy tone → restraint. Only isolated fragments → whole phrases + intentional breaths.

  • Clarinets Explained

Difficulty & suitability

G. Henle rates the Clarinet Sonata Op. 167 at level 7 (“difficult”) on its 1–9 scale, with ABRSM Grade 6 and Grade 8 cross-references depending on movement. The Allegretto is generally the most accessible of the four: challenges are sustained legato, tone control, dynamic shaping into the upper register, and chamber coordination in flowing 12/8 rather than extreme finger velocity (concentrated in the virtuosic finale). Advanced high-school players can approach movement I as refined legato and French-style study; college players use the full sonata as standard recital/jury repertoire. Teachers can use it for subdivision, phrase-building, dynamic pacing, and ensemble balance.
